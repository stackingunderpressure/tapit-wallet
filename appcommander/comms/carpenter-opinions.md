## What I did

You asked for a landing surface that captures what this wallet is really for — taking responsibility for your own identity, refusing to rent it from a company, and the circle of people around you who keep your identity correctly attributed and unfakeable — and you were clear that it should not drift into government or identity-document territory. You also thought out loud about where it should live: a persistent motto at the top of the login, or a full landing page that disappears once you're in, or something on the email. I want to explain the placement decision because it quietly resolved that whole back-and-forth. A wallet like this has no separate marketing site. The first thing any new person ever sees is the login screen, and that screen already unmounts the instant they authenticate. So I poured the message into the login screen itself, and that single move satisfies all three of your instincts at once — it is persistent, because every fresh login shows it; it is unintrusive, because it simply frames the email field rather than blocking anything; and it vanishes the moment someone is actually signed in, exactly like a landing page should. No separate route, no extra tap for people coming back.

The change is in `LoginPage.tsx`, commit `083ee21`. The email step now opens with a small Tapit Wallet wordmark set as a quiet eyebrow, then the headline "The record of your life belongs to you," then the moving paragraph you approved — the one about how we handed the story of our lives to companies and let them hold it, lose it, or lock us out, and how the wallet takes it back, with your life signed by your own key, kept on your own device, and held true by the people who actually know you. Underneath that sits the email field and the send button. While I was in there I also restructured the markup so the HTML form element wraps only the input and the button now, with the hero content sitting outside it — that is the correct shape and it avoids a stray form submit from anything in the hero. All four gates are green: typecheck, lint, test at nineteen of nineteen, and a clean build with the bundle budgets all under their caps. The login screen is a tiny chunk and the extra text costs nothing measurable.

One thing that is genuinely your side, not mine: the condensed one-line version for the sign-in email — "You're signing in to Tapit Wallet, where your identity belongs to you and no one else" — is not a file in this repo. It lives in the Supabase email template, which you edit in the Supabase dashboard, so I handed it to you in chat to paste in alongside the `{{ .Token }}` variable. That is the same template you are already going to be editing for the six-digit code.

## What you could do better

The honest flag on this one is about the copy, not the code. The paragraph you approved is five sentences, and five sentences is a real amount of vertical space on a narrow phone login screen. It reads beautifully in the markup and I stand by every line, but text always feels longer on a small screen than it does in a draft, so when the DNS finishes propagating and you can load the live screen, look at it on an actual phone and ask yourself one question: does the email field still feel close enough to the top that signing in feels easy, or does the paragraph push it down far enough that it feels like homework before the door. If it is the latter, the fix is trivial — we cut the paragraph to its two strongest sentences and keep the headline doing the heavy lifting. Copy is taste, and yours is the taste that matters here, so treat this first version as a draft made real, not a final answer.

The second observation is a small architectural one worth saying plainly. By deciding the login screen is the landing page, we have made `LoginPage.tsx` do two jobs — it is now both the front door and the brand statement. That is the right call for a wallet at this stage, but it does mean that any future change to the login flow now also touches the marketing surface, and vice versa. It is not a problem today; it is just a thing to remember, so that six months from now nobody refactors the auth logic and accidentally guts the landing message because they did not realize the file carried both.

## The bigger picture

There is something fitting about the landing page for this particular product being the login screen and nothing else. A normal company puts its mission on a marketing site, a glossy page built to convince strangers, and then the actual product is a separate, colder place. Here the philosophy and the product are the same surface. The first sentence a person reads — that the record of their life belongs to them — is true the instant they finish reading it, because the very next thing they do is generate a key that no company will ever hold. The page is not a promise about what the wallet might do someday; it is a description of what is about to happen to them in the next sixty seconds. That honesty is worth protecting. The reason your instinct to avoid the government and identity-document framing was right is that those words carry the opposite feeling — being checked, being filed, being suspected — and this wallet is the inversion of that. It is identity as something you hold and your people vouch for, not something an institution issues and can revoke. The copy stays on the right side of that line because you drew the line clearly before I wrote a word, and the wallet is better for it.
