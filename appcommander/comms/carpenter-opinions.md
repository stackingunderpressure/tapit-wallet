## What I did

You saw the landing copy go live, and your verdict was blunt and fair — very, very, very plain. The words were right but they were sitting on a flat field with nothing around them, and a sentence as serious as "the record of your life belongs to you" deserves a room with some architecture. So commit `ff5c37e` rebuilt the look of the login screen, which is also the wallet's landing page, and I want to walk you through what changed and why each piece earns its place.

The core move is a new `Frame` component that both the email step and the code step now render inside. The Frame is a shell with three layers. At the back is the warm paper field you already had. Floating on it are two large, very soft, heavily blurred colour glows — one in the forest-green accent, one in a warm amber — placed off the corners so they bleed gently around the card and drift, very slowly, so the screen feels alive rather than static. On top sits the real change: a frosted, elevated, rounded card with a soft deep shadow, and the whole login lives inside it now instead of floating loose on the background. The card is slightly translucent with a backdrop blur, so the colour glows behind it tint its edges faintly — that is where the "shading" you asked for comes from, real depth instead of flat paint. The card also rises in gently when the screen loads, a small piece of life at the threshold.

Inside the card, the headline is now set in a serif display face. This was a deliberate choice and a free one — I used the system serif stack, which means it costs zero network bytes and renders as New York on your iPhone, a genuinely beautiful face. A serif gives the headline gravity; it makes the sentence read like the first line of a charter rather than a form label, which is exactly the "changing the world" weight you were reaching for. The primary button became a green gradient with a colour-tinted shadow so it glows slightly off the card and presses down when tapped. The inputs got rounded corners and a soft focus ring. A gradient hairline divider, fading in from nothing at both ends, separates the story from the email field. And a small accent-dot wordmark sits up top. Everything stayed inside your existing paper, ink, and accent palette — I added no new brand colours, because the depth and warmth come from glow, elevation, gradient, and the serif face, not from a new coat of paint, and that keeps this screen unmistakably part of the same app as the wallet behind it. All four gates are green, and every bit of motion is wrapped so it switches off for anyone who has reduced-motion turned on.

## What you could do better

Here is the honest limitation, and it is the same one as last time but it matters more now: I cannot see this. Typecheck, lint, the test suite, and the build all passed, but not one of those four can render a pixel. Worse, Tailwind does not error on a misspelled class — it simply generates nothing — so a typo in a class name would slip through every gate and just quietly do nothing. I wrote every class carefully and they are all standard, but the only real verification is your eyes on the live deploy. So when this reaches your phone, look at three things specifically. One, the glow strength — on a bright phone outdoors the colour blobs might read as too subtle or, the other way, slightly muddy; tell me and I will dial the opacity. Two, the headline size — a serif at this size over three lines is intended to feel confident, but if it feels shouty, it comes down in seconds. Three, and this is the one I would bet on, the paragraph length — five sentences inside a card on a narrow phone is a lot of vertical reading before the email field, and now that the card has a fixed elegant frame around it, a too-long paragraph will feel more cramped than it did on the open page. If it feels heavy, the fix is to cut it to its two strongest sentences and let the serif headline and the glow carry the rest.

The second thing worth saying: I would genuinely benefit from a headless screenshot capability in this sandbox. Right now every visual change leans entirely on your phone loop, which works because you are fast and engaged, but it means I am shipping UI I have never seen. That is a gap in the tooling, not a gap in the work, and I have flagged it to the Foreman.

## The bigger picture

There is a reason a login screen deserves this much care, and it is not vanity. For most software the login is a toll booth — a thing to get through. For this wallet the login screen is the first and possibly only piece of marketing the product will ever have, because there is no separate website, and it is also the threshold of a genuinely serious act: the next sixty seconds generate a key that no company will ever hold. A plain screen quietly tells a person that nothing important is happening here. An elegant one, with weight and warmth and a sentence set like a charter, tells them the truth — that they are about to take something back. The design is not decoration on top of the meaning; it is the meaning, made visible before the user has read a word. You were right that plain was wrong, and you were right to push for life. The wallet should feel, at its front door, like it knows what it is for.
