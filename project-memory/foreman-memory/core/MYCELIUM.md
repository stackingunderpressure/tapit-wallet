# MYCELIUM.md — the network spec

*Companion to THE_THESIS.md and HEARTH_SPEC.md. This document describes the network layer — how Hearths connect, how trust flows between them, how recipes propagate, and how identity grows from a single keypair into a verified place in the larger forest. Written 2026-05-09. Mycelial language threaded through because the architecture genuinely is mycelial in shape.*

---

## What the Mycelium is

The Mycelium is the network of Hearths. If a Hearth is a single node where one operator's engine, memory, and identity live, the Mycelium is the underground that connects all of those Hearths to each other through cryptographic hyphae carrying signed attestations and recipe spores. Each Hearth is a fruiting body the world sees above ground; the Mycelium is the durable thing underneath that holds the forest together. You connect to the Mycelium through your Hearth. You don't log into the Mycelium because there is no central server to log into. You grow into it, and it grows into you, slowly, through real relationships verified by math.

The biological pattern is not metaphor here. It is a working description of the architecture. Suzanne Simard's research on the wood-wide-web showed that mature trees in healthy forests share carbon, nitrogen, and stress signals through underground mycelial networks, with older trees feeding seedlings and stressed trees alerting their neighbors. The largest known organism on Earth is a mycelium in eastern Oregon spanning twenty-four hundred acres and possibly eight thousand years old. Mycelium is what life on this planet uses for distributed cooperation at scale, and the patterns it has evolved are exactly the patterns this architecture needs. We are porting a four-billion-year-old design into a substrate that did not have it before.

## How the architecture maps to the biology

Every Hearth is a fruiting body. The mushroom is what people see and what they use. It is the visible product, the moment of emergence above ground, the part you reach for. But the mushroom is temporary, while the mycelium underneath persists across seasons and storms. In the same way, each app a Hearth fruits is temporary by design, replaceable by a better recipe whenever one arrives, but the Hearth itself and its place in the Mycelium endures.

Recipes are spores. A recipe leaves its originating Hearth as a small, signed, self-contained packet describing what an app does and how to rebuild it. Spores drift through the network along trust paths, find Hearths with compatible substrate, and take root there as new fruiting bodies, each one re-personalized to the local soil chemistry. One recipe, infinite descendants, none identical, all related to the original through the cryptographic lineage. This is the Boundaries Doctrine generalized to the network layer. Receivers re-implement clean through their engine; they do not copy bytes.

Attestations are hyphal connections. When Alice's Hearth signs an attestation about Bob's Hearth, that signed claim is a hyphal thread connecting two organisms, carrying nutrients in both directions. The thread is tamper-evident because the signature can be verified, but it is also socially earned because Alice had to actually know Bob to write the attestation honestly. Hyphae grow slowly. Real ones do, too.

Decomposition is honest. When a recipe stops being useful, its learnings break down and feed back into the soil as patterns and primitives that future recipes inherit without copying directly. AppCommander's existing pattern of writing comms records and opinions documents at session end is the same shape — finished work decomposes into doctrine, doctrine feeds the next session. Nothing goes to waste because the network is mutualist, not extractive.

Symbiosis is the basic shape of every relationship in this network. A mycorrhizal partnership in biology is the deep collaboration between a fungus and a tree, where each gives what the other cannot make. In the Mycelium, mycorrhizal partnerships are the deep trust relationships between specific Hearths. Bree's Hearth and her sister's Hearth, if they share recipes and attestations consistently, are mycorrhizal partners. The relationship is mutual, signed, durable, and visible to the network as a high-confidence bridge.

## The five identity layers, growing tap-root style

Identity in the Mycelium grows like a tree's root system. The taproot goes deep first, supporting a sapling. Lateral roots branch out as the tree matures, each one a relationship to surrounding soil. Eventually the root system is wide, deep, and integrated with the forest's mycelial network, and the tree is held up by everything around it as much as by its own taproot.

The first layer is the taproot itself. Every Hearth generates an Ed25519 keypair on its first run. The public key is the Hearth's identity in the network. The private key stays in the Hearth, never leaves, signs everything outbound. The operator writes their own profile across three tiers, public for anything safe to hand out, project for scoped working contexts, and sensitive for things that should not leave the Hearth without deliberate export. At this layer the Hearth could claim to be anyone; the substrate does not police self-claims because no one else can, either. This is the necessary cryptographic anchor everything else grows from, but a sapling cannot hold itself up on the taproot alone.

The second layer is the mycorrhizal partnerships. Your closest five to twenty Hearths, the ones run by people who actually know you in person, sign attestations confirming you are who your profile says you are. Each attestation is small, signed, timestamped, anchored, and stored in both Hearths. The federation layer of the spec exposes the primitive; the human work is the friendship that produces honest attestations. PGP's web-of-trust failed because it asked humans to do too much manual cryptographic work, but with Frank handling protocol details quietly behind the scenes, the operator only has to add a friend to their peer list. The math handles the rest.

The third layer is the hyphal lattice of transitive trust. If Alice trusts Bob, and Bob trusts Carol, then Alice can reach Carol along a verified two-hop path with reduced confidence. Each hop discounts the trust score, but the geometry of the lattice itself becomes information. Three independent paths from Alice to Carol all agreeing is much stronger than one. The Mycelium does not maintain a master view of the trust graph because no master view exists; instead, every Hearth maintains its own local view of the lattice as it has explored it, refreshed by federation queries.

The fourth layer is the anchoring rings. Every meaningful action a Hearth takes — shipping a recipe, completing a transaction, producing a verified piece of work, sending an attestation — produces a signed receipt. Receipts are batched and anchored to a public timeline outside the network's control, so they cannot be rewritten later even by the Hearth that produced them. The simplest anchor today is OpenTimestamps over the Bitcoin chain, which uses Bitcoin's tamper-evidence as a public clock without requiring the Hearth to spend bitcoin or run a node. Over time, your Hearth's anchored history becomes its growth rings, durable and auditable, the same way a tree's annual rings are durable and auditable. Other Hearths can verify consistency without trusting the originator's claims; they can read the math directly.

The fifth layer is the forest consensus. After enough years and enough verified history, the network as a whole reaches a soft consensus that you are real, you are consistent, and you have shown up for the people in your local lattice in a way that makes you a known organism in the larger forest. This is what the operator means by the heavyweight of society agreeing that you are who you are. It is not a moment of identification. It is a slow accumulation that becomes undeniable through receipts no one can fake. This layer cannot be shipped on day one because it does not exist on day one; it emerges from years of sustained operation by Hearths that started with layers one through four. The earliest Hearths in the network do the most work earning the foundational reputation, and later Hearths inherit a substrate that is already strong.

## Federation, kept simple

Federation is the layer that turns isolated Hearths into a working network. It has three sub-protocols, all of which start small and stay small for the first thousand Hearths. The thirty-year history of decentralized social and computing failures is largely a history of trying to solve federation too completely on first ship; this spec takes the opposite approach and ships the smallest things that work.

Discovery, in v1, is manual. The operator shares their Hearth's public address with a friend through any out-of-band channel they trust — text, email, in person, paper. The friend's Hearth fetches the address, retrieves the public key, and adds it to the peer list. There is no directory, no search, no friend recommender. Future versions can layer in friend-of-friend introduction signed by the introducing Hearth, then much later something like reputation-based directories, but discovery does not need to be solved on day one. The first thousand Hearths will all know each other through real-world relationships, and that is fine.

Trust, in v1, is a direct list. Each Hearth has its own set of peers it has explicitly added. Recipes from listed peers are accepted; recipes from unlisted peers are rejected entirely. This is brutally simple but it works because it matches how humans actually decide who to trust. Future versions can layer in transitive trust scoring, where my friend's trusted peers count for something even though I have not added them directly, and eventually reputation-based trust for the broader Mycelium. PGP's web-of-trust failed because it asked humans to do too much; this spec stays simple and lets the system grow only as the math actually needs it to.

Recipe propagation has two patterns and both are supported. Pull is when a receiver fetches a recipe URL the originator shared. Push is when the originator's Hearth announces "I just published recipe X" to its trusted peers' Hearths and those Hearths fetch it on receipt. Pull is simpler; push is friendlier. Both run on the same primitive of signed manifests + verified signatures, and either can be the primary path depending on how the operator and their peers prefer to work.

## Privacy is in what you do not anchor

The cryptography is in service of privacy, not against it. Anchor the proof of an event without anchoring the content of the event. The hash of a claim is anchored, not the claim itself; the world can verify that you said something on a particular date without seeing what you said until you choose to reveal. Attestations are stored in both endpoints and never broadcast publicly; the Mycelium knows the connection exists if it traces the lattice, but the content of the attestation stays private to the two parties. State snapshots of a Hearth are anchored as hashes; the actual state stays in the Hearth. The pattern is standard zero-knowledge commitment territory, used here because it lets the network maintain integrity without forcing operators to publish their lives. Your tap root anchors are public; the soil around them is yours.

## How AppCommander already implements this

The Mycelium is not a future system. AppCommander already runs most of it under different names. The Hearth's identity layer is the operator's profile plus the keypair behind Supabase auth. The storage layer is Supabase plus GitHub repos per project. The engine layer is the foreman edge function plus dispatch-carpenter plus the Anthropic Claude calls. Recipes are the Frank skeletons in the appcommander/frank-skeletons folder, which are already portable manifests that get re-personalized by the bootstrap-project edge function whenever a new project is spawned. The federation layer is the missing piece in v1 of AppCommander, and exposing federation endpoints is the next big build.

Naming the layers of what AppCommander is already doing turns it from "the operator's project tool" into "the reference Hearth of the open Mycelium." The work to elevate it formally is mostly done. The doctrine just has to catch up to the code.

## The build order

The next year of work happens in a deliberate sequence. First the doctrine documents are committed and the architecture is named, which has happened tonight with THE_THESIS, HEARTH_SPEC, this MYCELIUM.md, and the coming HEARTWOOD.md governance document. Second is layer one identity formalized — the keypair pattern made explicit and the operator profile structured into the three tiers. Third is layer two attestations, which means giving Hearths the federation primitive of "sign and announce a vouching claim about a peer," and the storage to hold incoming attestations from peers about you. Fourth is recipe export and import, which is the smallest demonstration that the substrate works, with one recipe traveling from your Hearth to Bree's Hearth and re-personalizing through her engine. Fifth is OpenTimestamp anchoring of receipts, which begins the layer four growth-ring accumulation. Layer three transitive trust queries and layer five forest consensus emerge naturally from sustained operation across the first four; they are not separate ships, they are what happens to the network when the first four run for years.

## How other builders plug in

The architecture is open by design. The operator's stated role is architect-and-steward, not founder-CEO, and the Mycelium spec is the seam where other builders add their own layers. Three explicit invitations stand from day one. Build an alternative Hearth — AppCommander is the reference, but another builder might want a different cockpit, a different feature set, a different domain focus, and as long as the Hearth implements the five-layer interface from HEARTH_SPEC.md, it is a valid node on the Mycelium. Build a layer — pick storage, engine, federation, identity, or recipes, and ship a polished alternative implementation that operators can swap into their Hearth. Build a tool — profile editors, recipe browsers, federation visualizers, trust-graph monitors, hub-health dashboards, anything that plugs into the spec's primitives without being a Hearth itself. The expectation is that within a year of THE_THESIS being public, alternative implementations of every layer exist. The architecture only succeeds if the operator is not the only builder.
