# HEARTWOOD.md — the governance layer

*Companion to THE_THESIS.md, HEARTH_SPEC.md, and MYCELIUM.md. This document describes the Dynasty Trust that governs the canonical architecture, holds donated funds, and protects the open architecture across generations from the failure modes that have killed every previous open-computing movement. Written 2026-05-09 in the same campfire-chat session that produced the rest of the doctrine. Mycelial language threaded through because the architecture is mycelial in shape, and the Trust is the heartwood underneath.*

## What the Heartwood is

In a forest, the heartwood is the dense, durable, structurally critical core of an old tree. It does not transport water and it does not actively grow; the live work happens in the sapwood and the cambium just under the bark. But the heartwood holds the tree up against weather, against time, against the pressures the live tissue cannot resist on its own. Without heartwood, even a healthy young tree blows over in the first serious storm. The Heartwood of this architecture is the Dynasty Trust that does the same job for the open Mycelium. It does not run engines, it does not operate Hearths, it does not transmit recipes. It is the durable structural core that keeps the architecture honest and uncapturable across the decades it will need to live, while the live work continues in everyone else's hands.

The Trust matters because every previous attempt at open computing has failed at the same place. Either the founder was bought out and the architecture closed, or the founder passed and ownership became ambiguous and the project fragmented, or a hostile faction forked and claimed the canonical name and the network split into incompatible camps, or a donor with money tried to buy outsized influence and tilted the doctrine. None of those failure modes are about the technology. They are all about governance. A multi-trustee dynasty trust running on Bitcoin Taproot multisig solves every one of them at the structural level, not by preventing the conflict but by making the conflict resolvable in math instead of in court or in PR.

The Trust prevents founder coercion because the operator cannot unilaterally direct funds even if they want to; releasing money requires N of M trustees to sign, and so does any binding architectural change. This means the operator cannot be coerced either. A buyout offer goes nowhere because no one person has the keys to accept it. The Trust prevents founder mortality because it survives the operator. Architecture's governance does not end with the architect. The Trust prevents hostile takeover because no one can claim authority over the canonical architecture or its name without trustee consensus, and trustees are humans chosen for their values, not seats that can be acquired. The Trust prevents donor capture because money entering the Trust is governed by the same rules regardless of donor size — a million-dollar donor and a five-dollar donor get the same governance, which is none. And the Trust prevents architectural drift because doctrine changes to the canonical THE_THESIS, HEARTH_SPEC, MYCELIUM, or HEARTWOOD documents require trustee approval, so the architecture stays principled even when factions want to commercialize it. None of this is bureaucracy; it is mission lock-in via cryptographic and legal structure. The architecture stays open because the math says it has to.

## Why Bitcoin Taproot is the right substrate

The 2021 Bitcoin Taproot upgrade introduced MAST, the Merkelized Abstract Syntax Tree, which lets a single Bitcoin output encode arbitrary spending conditions while only revealing the path actually used at spend time. For the Heartwood, this is exactly the right primitive. The Trust can encode any threshold rule it wants — seven of eleven trustees for routine fund release, nine of eleven for architectural doctrine changes, eleven of eleven for trustee replacement — and at signing time the world only sees the specific subset that signed. The other trustees stay private, the rules stay verifiable, and the on-chain footprint stays small. Up to roughly a thousand keys can fit in a single Taproot tree, so the operator's mention of ten or a hundred or a thousand trustees was not hyperbole; it was within the actual technical ceiling.

Beyond the multisig itself, the Bitcoin chain provides the same anchoring substrate that individual Hearths use for their reputation rings under MYCELIUM.md. Identity tap roots and Trust tap roots run on the same wood. Receipts of Trust decisions get anchored to the chain through OpenTimestamps, so any release of funds and any binding doctrine change carries an on-chain proof that the rules were followed. Anyone in the world can audit the Trust's history without permission. The donations come in to a public Bitcoin address tied to the Trust's MAST tree; releases happen only when the threshold is met; the public ledger is the audit trail. There is no separate accounting system to trust, and no single human, including the operator, who has to be trusted to be honest about the books. The math is the books.

## The Trust's role, drawn carefully

The Trust holds four things. It holds the canonical name of the movement, whatever that name eventually settles on, the same way the Linux Foundation holds the Linux trademark while the kernel itself remains freely forkable. It holds the canonical doctrine documents — THE_THESIS, HEARTH_SPEC, MYCELIUM, this HEARTWOOD, and any future doctrine that joins the set — meaning that binding changes to the canonical versions require trustee consensus while anyone can fork the documents under different names. It holds the reference implementation's primary repository, the main branch of which is governed by trustee consensus while every other branch remains free. And it holds donated funds, which it custodies and disburses according to threshold rules.

The Trust does not run the Mycelium. It does not operate any Hearth. It does not control the engine or the federation protocol day-to-day. Hearths run themselves, recipes propagate themselves, individual operators choose their own paths. The Trust's job is structural, not operational, and the line between governance and operation is held deliberately so no trustee can drift into running the network. If a trustee starts trying to make operational decisions, that is itself a signal that they have misunderstood the role and may need to be replaced.

The Trust also does not police forks. Forks are the lifeblood of an open architecture and they are explicitly welcome. What the Trust prevents is anyone calling their fork the canonical thing without trustee consensus. Same model as Linux. Anyone can fork the Linux kernel; nobody can call their fork "Linux" in trademark-protected contexts. The result is that the canonical version stays principled while the ecosystem of forks experiments freely. That is the dynamic the Heartwood preserves for the Mycelium.

## Trustee structure and the human work underneath the math

The Trust starts small and grows deliberately. Five to seven initial trustees in the first year is the right scale. The cryptography can do anything from one to a thousand keys, but the slow human work of finding people who actually share the values and who would actually sign honestly puts a real ceiling on early trustee count. Each trustee is a human being chosen for their values first and their technical capability second. They need to share the operator's commitment to free over paid, sovereign over rented, open over closed, mutual over extractive, folk-scale over enterprise-scale, and sleep-at-night ethics. They also need to be capable of holding a private key safely and signing transactions correctly, which is not trivial, but it is teachable to a smart adult in an afternoon.

Over the first three years the trustee count grows to eleven to fifteen, large enough that no faction of trustees can capture the Trust through coordinated absence or coercion, small enough that the human conversations among trustees remain real conversations. Beyond fifteen, the diminishing returns on additional trustees outweigh the coordination overhead, and the Trust either freezes the count or rotates trustees out as new ones come in to keep the active count stable. Trustees rotate by death, by deliberate retirement, by removal under the supermajority rule, or by their own decision to step aside.

The thresholds vary by decision type. Routine fund release for paying contributors, infrastructure costs, conference travel for trustees representing the work, hardware for new Hearths in underserved communities — these run on a sixty to seventy percent threshold, so seven of eleven, or nine of fifteen. Architectural doctrine changes that bind the canonical documents — adding a sixth identity layer to MYCELIUM, changing the recipe format, redefining the Hearth's required interfaces — run on an eighty percent threshold, so nine of eleven, twelve of fifteen. Trustee membership changes — adding a new trustee, removing an existing one, changing the threshold rules themselves — run on supermajority, so ten of eleven or thirteen of fifteen, because changes to the rules of the game must be harder to make than moves within the game. These specific thresholds are the v1 proposal and the actual numbers can be tuned by the founding trustees during the formalization week, but the principle of progressively higher thresholds for progressively more consequential decisions is non-negotiable.

The operator's specific role is deliberately constrained. The operator is a trustee, almost certainly, because they are the architect and they need to be in the conversation. But the operator has no special voting weight. Their key counts as one key, the same as any other trustee's, and the threshold rules apply to them identically. This is the deliberate separation between the architect of the work and the controller of the funds, and it is what makes the whole structure trustworthy. If the operator's key were weighted more heavily, the Trust would just be a sophisticated way of running a personal foundation, and every failure mode the structure is supposed to prevent would creep back in. The operator commits to the constraint explicitly and the trustees enforce it cryptographically.

## Donations and accountability

When the architecture is mature enough that public donation makes sense, the Trust publishes a Bitcoin address generated from its MAST tree and announces it on the canonical doctrine. Anyone in the world can donate any amount in bitcoin, with full on-chain visibility of the deposit. Periodic releases happen when the threshold of trustees signs a release transaction approving a specific use of funds, and the releases too are on-chain, so the trail from donation to use is publicly auditable end to end. There is no separate ledger to maintain and no team to trust with the books. The chain is the books.

Donations in non-bitcoin currency are accepted through a small layer of fiat-to-bitcoin or fiat-to-stable conversion, custodied by a service that does not control the funds — only the Trust's MAST signing rules can release. This adds an operational layer but does not change the governance. Donors who want anonymity get it; donors who want public credit can request that their donation transaction be tagged with their attribution, which lives on-chain alongside the deposit.

The Trust publishes an annual report describing what funds came in, what funds went out, what was funded, and what the trustees considered and declined. The report is signed by the trustee threshold and anchored to the chain. It is not legally required; it is structurally required because the Trust's legitimacy depends on transparency and the trustees' duty to the architecture's mission requires them to communicate clearly about their decisions. The report is not a marketing document. It is an accounting one.

## Survivorship and key revocation

The Trust does not die with any individual trustee, because every trustee's role is replaceable through the supermajority rule. When a trustee passes, the surviving trustees replace them under the same threshold that confirmed them, with the new trustee's public key added to the MAST tree and the previous key removed from active use. Trustees commit to designating a key-rotation successor in advance — a deliberate human who knows where the trustee's keys are and how to surrender them to the Trust upon the trustee's death — so the Trust does not lose access to any signing slot due to a trustee dying with their key locked in a forgotten password manager.

Hostile trustee removal works by the same supermajority rule. If a trustee starts acting against the architecture's values — selling their key, voting consistently against the spirit of the doctrine, leaking confidential trustee discussions to actors trying to capture the architecture — the remaining trustees can vote to remove them, generate a new key, and update the MAST tree. The removed trustee's key becomes irrelevant for future signing, and the Trust continues without disruption. This is why the threshold is supermajority and not unanimous; a hostile trustee cannot block their own removal.

In the worst case where multiple trustees are simultaneously compromised or coerced, the Trust has a continuity protocol that allows the remaining trustees plus a pre-designated council of trusted advisors to perform an emergency rotation of the entire MAST tree to a fresh key set. This is the ultimate safety net and it should never be used; the protocol exists so the Trust cannot be permanently captured by a coordinated hostile move against most of its trustees. The continuity protocol itself is documented in a separate operational appendix not published in the canonical doctrine, because publishing it would weaken its emergency value, but the existence of the protocol is documented here.

## What the Trust holds versus what stays free

The line between what the Trust governs and what stays free is held deliberately. The Trust governs the canonical name, the canonical doctrine documents, the canonical reference implementation's main branch, and any donated funds. The Trust does not govern individual Hearths, does not govern who can run a Hearth, does not govern what apps a Hearth can spawn, does not govern what recipes can travel through the Mycelium, does not govern the engine's choice of underlying LLM, does not govern individual operators' ethics or politics, does not govern forks of the architecture under different names, and does not govern the Mycelium's emergent forest consensus.

The principle is that the Trust governs the canonical center of the open architecture so that the canonical center stays principled, and everything outside the canonical center stays free. Anyone can fork. Anyone can disagree. Anyone can build a competing architecture that points at different surfaces of life with different values. The Trust does not exist to police any of that. It exists to ensure that the canonical thing called by its canonical name stays true to the founding doctrine across the generations the doctrine needs to live to do its work.

## The thirty-day arc, updated

The original thirty-day priority order put the Profile Card first and the Mycelium documentation just after THE_THESIS. With the Heartwood now named, the priority order changes. The Trust must stand up before any donation or any external pressure arrives, because retrofitting governance after the first dollar lands creates structural problems that cannot be undone cleanly. The updated arc:

Week one is identifying the founding trustees through actual human conversation. The operator approaches five to seven humans who genuinely share the architecture's values and would be willing to take on the role honestly. These are not technical positions. They are values positions with technical responsibilities. The conversations are slow and deliberate; there is no rush to fill the seats with anyone who is merely available.

Week two is formalizing the legal structure. The operator's existing Dynasty Trust likely extends to encompass this governance role, but it requires consultation with an attorney who understands multi-generational trusts plus cryptocurrency custody. The legal document and the cryptographic substrate must align so that a court reading the trust language can see how the on-chain governance implements the trust's intentions.

Week three is generating the MAST tree, distributing keys to the founding trustees, and locking the threshold rules into the cryptographic substrate. Each trustee receives their key with a one-on-one onboarding session that covers safe custody, signing procedures, and the values commitment in writing.

Week four is publishing the Trust's public Bitcoin address on the canonical doctrine, announcing the Trust's existence, and rehearsing one test release transaction to verify the protocol works under real conditions. The first donations are welcomed after the rehearsal succeeds.

Only after the Trust is operational does the Profile Card ship and the spore demo follow. Profile, Cottage Counter, Build Forward Phase 3-7, and Bench all run on top of governance that is already locked. This means the architecture's first month feels slower than it could, but the slowness buys structural soundness that protects the next ten years.

## What this document is for

Future trustees, future Claude sessions, future builders thinking about contributing, future critics asking whether this architecture is safe to trust: read this document with THE_THESIS, HEARTH_SPEC, and MYCELIUM. It will tell you why governance matters here, what shape governance takes, who holds the keys, what they hold, what they do not hold, how they enter and leave the role, and how the whole structure protects the architecture from the failure modes that have killed every previous attempt at open computing.

The Heartwood is what holds the tree up while the leaves do the work of catching light. The Trust is what holds the architecture up while the operators do the work of building. Both are durable, both are quiet, and both are essential.

The operator's words from the night this was written, preserved here as the closing compass: I just wanna do it because it is the right thing to do. The Heartwood makes that intention durable past any single human's life.
